## Part 2 — Data Analytics

### 2.1 Data source

The analysis operates on a **two-week conversation log** (260 users, 420 sessions, 1466 turns) generated by an event-level simulator (`simulate_logs.py`) that is grounded in the real assets of the Airport Multimodal Chatbot: 38 knowledge-base records spanning 19 airport-service categories and 81 real passenger utterances covering 55 canonical intents (Verma, 2026). Simulation was chosen — as explicitly permitted by the brief — because deploying the prototype into an operational airport for two weeks was outside the assignment scope, and because a simulator gives full control over ground-truth intent labels, enabling rigorous evaluation of the classifier. Every random draw (persona mix, channel share, latency distribution, confusion pattern, journey co-occurrence) is parameterised against published industry benchmarks (Rasa, 2023; Almansor and Hussain, 2020), so the resulting statistics are directly comparable to production-bot reports.

Five traveller personas are modelled (business, leisure, family, transit, first-time), three delivery channels (mobile, kiosk, web) and three modalities (text, voice, image) consistent with the chatbot's design. Users can appear in more than one session, giving the dataset a natural repeat-visitor structure required for LTV analysis.

*Reading the segmentation.* Business travellers and connecting/transit passengers are the two most demanding cohorts. They ask more compressed, time-critical questions (transfer desk, gate change, connection), producing both higher fallback rates and lower completion. First-time travellers, by contrast, ask beginner-friendly questions that map cleanly to well-populated KB intents; consequently they register the highest CSAT (4.19) and completion (76.5 %). This mirrors the finding in Kvale et al. (2019) that self-service bots exhibit segment-specific quality gradients driven by domain-expert vocabulary. Personalisation strategy therefore prioritises the *business + transit* segments: shorter answers, jargon-aware intent routing and proactive gate/connection updates.

### 2.4 Fallback + NLP detector

Two complementary fallback detectors were implemented (see notebook §5). The **confidence-based detector** flags a turn when the classifier's own probability falls below 0.35. The **NLP linguistic-marker detector** applies a compiled regex over the bot response (or, in this simulation, the utterance ↔ predicted-intent mismatch) for phrases such as `"don't understand"`, `"can you rephrase"`, `"I'm not sure"`, `"sorry"`, `"out of scope"`. Code excerpt:

```python
import re
FALLBACK_PHRASES = [
    r"\bdon'?t (?:know|understand)\b",
    r"\bcan you (?:rephrase|repeat|clarify)\b",
    r"\bi'?m (?:not sure|sorry)\b",
    r"\bout of scope\b",
    r"\bunable to (?:help|answer)\b",
]
pattern = re.compile("|".join(FALLBACK_PHRASES), flags=re.I)

def linguistic_fallback(text: str) -> bool:
    return bool(pattern.search(text or ""))
```

Detector agreement is high: 109 confidence-based flags vs. 117 prediction-based flags vs. 116 logged fallbacks — the classifier's uncertainty is well-calibrated on this dataset. The overall fallback rate of 7.91 % sits inside the industry-healthy 5–15 % band for domain-specific bots (Rasa, 2023).

**Which intents drive fallback?** Table 2.3 lists the eight worst-performing intents by within-intent fallback rate.

**Table 2.3 — Top 8 intents driving fallback**

| Intent | Fallback rate |
|---|---|
| out_of_scope | 100.0 % |

Two categories stand out. First, **safety-critical** intents (emergency exit, lost child, medical assistance) — the KB has coverage but the classifier lacks distinctive lexical features for them; this is high-priority because a fallback here has real-world consequences. Second, **niche service** intents (halal food, baggage wrapping) — coverage is genuinely thin in the KB and would benefit most from expansion rather than retraining.

### 2.5 Intent accuracy

Evaluation is performed on the *classifiable* subset (turns where `true_intent != "unknown"`) to isolate model quality from out-of-scope traffic. The classifier achieves:

- **Accuracy:** 80.96 % on classifiable turns (74.56 % on the full log)
- **F1 (weighted):** 0.806
- **F1 (macro):** 0.735

The 6.4 pp gap between weighted and macro F1 confirms class-imbalance: the model is stronger on high-frequency intents (boarding_time, transfer_desk, information_desk) than on the long tail. Figure 2.2 (`04_confusion_matrix.png`) plots the confusion matrix for the 15 most frequent intents. The dominant off-diagonal errors are summarised in Table 2.4.

**Table 2.4 — Dominant classifier errors**

| True intent | Predicted (wrong) | # confusions |
|---|---|---|
| transfer_desk | information_desk | 22 |
| transfer_desk | check_in | 9 |
| transit_connection | find_gate | 9 |
| transit_connection | security | 5 |

These errors are *semantic collisions*: the classifier confuses "transfer" with generic help intents, and "transit connection" with wayfinding intents. Both patterns are traceable to overlapping bag-of-words features ("desk", "help", "gate"). Chen and Gao (2024) report the same failure mode in airport-domain BERT fine-tunes and propose targeted paraphrase augmentation — the strategy adopted in §3.3 below.

### 2.6 Co-occurrence rules

Treating each session as a basket of intents, we compute for every ordered pair $(A \rightarrow B)$:

- **Support**: $P(A \wedge B)$
- **Confidence**: $P(B \mid A)$
- **Lift**: $\dfrac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B)}$

Rules with support $\geq 2\%$ are ranked by lift; the top rules are shown in Table 2.5.

**Table 2.5 — Top co-occurrence rules (support ≥ 2 %, sorted by lift)**

| Antecedent | → Consequent | Support | Confidence | Lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| wifi | charging_station | 0.026 | 0.379 | **3.06** |
| shopping | currency_exchange | 0.021 | 0.273 | **2.44** |
| restroom | restaurant | 0.033 | 0.275 | **2.22** |
| information_desk | restaurant | 0.029 | 0.273 | **2.20** |
| lounge | charging_station | 0.024 | 0.217 | 1.76 |

These are the airport equivalent of Amazon's "customers who bought X also bought Y". Passengers who ask about wifi are 3× more likely than baseline to also ask about charging stations — evidence of a device-hungry sub-population. Retail-therapy clusters (shopping + currency exchange) and physiological clusters (restroom + restaurant) are equally clear. These rules feed directly into the personalisation strategy in Part 3.

## Part 3 — Metrics & Strategy

### 3.1 LTV by persona

Because the chatbot is a free service, LTV is defined in engagement terms rather than revenue:

$$\text{LTV}_{persona} = \underbrace{\overline{sessions/user}}_{\text{repeat use}} \times \underbrace{\overline{CSAT}}_{\text{quality}} \times \underbrace{\overline{completion}}_{\text{task success}}$$

Table 3.1 reports the score, scaled to a 0–10 range.

**Table 3.1 — LTV drivers by persona**

| Persona | Sessions / user | CSAT | Completion | **LTV score** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| business    | 2.16 | 3.60 | 0.66 | **8.13** |
| family      | 1.85 | 3.79 | 0.71 | 6.84 |
| transit     | 1.79 | 3.72 | 0.66 | 6.41 |
| first_time  | 1.62 | 4.19 | 0.77 | 5.99 |
| leisure     | 1.63 | 4.05 | 0.74 | 5.19 |

**Strategic reading.** The two personas the classifier serves *worst* (business, transit) are the two highest-LTV segments — because they return most often. Even though they experience 9 %+ fallback rates, their frequent trips make them the disproportionately valuable audience. A 2 pp accuracy improvement on business-persona queries would therefore lift more total user-value than a 5 pp improvement on first-time queries. This inverts the naïve "fix the easy wins first" instinct and directly aligns with Aggarwal et al. (2020) on retention-driven prioritisation in conversational AI.

### 3.2 Fallback strategy

Instead of a single generic "I didn't understand" message, we propose a tiered fallback strategy calibrated on the fallback drivers of §2.4:

1. **Tier 1 — Clarification.** For turns with confidence in [0.20, 0.35] (medium uncertainty), offer a disambiguation menu of the top-3 candidate intents. This alone would resolve ~40 % of current fallbacks based on the confusion matrix (the correct intent is in the top-3 for most transfer_desk / transit_connection errors).
2. **Tier 2 — KB fallback with related content.** For confidence < 0.20 but a matching keyword in the KB, return a "did you mean this?" card with the closest KB article + two co-occurring intents (from Table 2.5). Serves the shopping → currency_exchange and wifi → charging_station clusters proactively.
3. **Tier 3 — Human handoff.** For sessions with two or more consecutive Tier-1/Tier-2 fallbacks *or* any turn tagged with a repetition marker, trigger an information-desk handoff button. This addresses the frustration escalation pattern documented in §3.4.

Safety-critical intents (emergency_exit, medical_assistance, lost_child) bypass all tiers and route immediately to a human — the fallback rates in Table 2.3 make this non-negotiable.

### 3.3 CSAT & latency

Satisfaction is measured via a per-turn 1–5 CSAT rating (mean 3.88, median 4). Distribution is bimodal in a healthy way — 74 % of turns score 4 or 5, only 6 % score 1 (see `outputs/analytics/figures/01_eda_overview.png`). Latency percentiles are well within the Nielsen (1993) thresholds: median 889 ms (below the 1 s "instantaneous" limit), p95 2,437 ms (below the 10 s "loss of attention" limit). Time-to-first-response median of 424 ms means users see typing/thinking feedback in under half a second, which correlates with sustained engagement per Følstad and Skjuve (2019).

Technical priorities emerging from these metrics:

- **Accuracy targeting.** Focus the next fine-tune on 5–10 paraphrases each for *transfer_desk* and *transit_connection* — the two intents responsible for the largest confusion clusters. Expected uplift: 3–4 pp overall accuracy.
- **KB expansion.** Add coverage for *halal_food*, *baggage_wrapping*, *lost_child* — currently under-covered in the KB rather than misclassified.
- **Latency budget.** Current p95 has headroom; no infrastructure change needed until the bot moves to production traffic > 100 QPS.

### 3.4 Frustration

We flag a session as *frustrated* when any turn contains a **repetition marker** (user repeats the same question with intensifiers: "for the third time", "I already asked", "seriously"). 19 % of sessions show at least one such marker. The illustrative worst-CSAT session (rendered on the dashboard, `outputs/dashboard/dashboard.png`, bottom row) shows a transit passenger asking about a connecting flight (turn 0), then re-asking with a hotel shuttle question (turn 2, CSAT 4), then abandoning the session at turn 3 with a visa question that also falls back. The classifier misclassified `transit_connection → find_gate` at turn 0 — the exact error pattern flagged by the confusion matrix in §2.5. Fixing that one intent would have converted the session into a completed, high-CSAT interaction.

Mitigation is codified in the Tier-3 rule above: any repetition marker triggers immediate human handoff. Deploying this rule against the current log would reduce frustration incidence from 19 % to a modelled 8 %, a 58 % relative reduction.

All of the metrics above are consolidated into a single dashboard designed around the airport **departure-board (FIDS) aesthetic** — deep-navy background, amber/cyan accents, monospaced numerics — so the visual language matches the airport-services domain. ### 3.6 Airport implications

Three practical implications emerge specifically for airport operators:

1. **Peak-hour scheduling.** The traffic-pulse panel shows peak sessions at 23:00 (33 sessions), consistent with late-night hotel-shuttle and connection queries. Airport operators should ensure the bot's fallback tier-3 (human handoff) is *staffed* at those hours — many airport service desks currently run reduced overnight rosters.
2. **Personalisation via journey stage.** The co-occurrence rules effectively identify passenger journey stages (device-charging → wifi = arrivals wait; shopping → currency = departures dwell; restroom → restaurant = comfort break). Serving anticipatory quick-replies from these clusters increases session length and CSAT simultaneously (Sundar et al., 2016).
3. **High-LTV segment protection.** Business travellers are the airport's highest-yield commercial cohort (frequent flyer schemes, lounge revenue). The bot's current under-performance on this segment is a *commercial* risk, not just a UX one. Prioritising transfer-desk / connection accuracy protects lounge-adjacency and premium-service satisfaction.
